The mechanism

A layer is additive. A coupling is not.

The reason "trajectory navigation" reads as just another layer is that, as specified, it is one. Three modules that each optimise their own objective and hand a result downstream compose into a chain — and a chain decomposes, which means a cross-heuristic solves it, which means RL is unjustified. Correctly.

But the physics is not a chain, and it is not a single loop either. It runs on two clocks — a plan-time loop that commits before takeoff, and a flight-time loop that reacts at every stop — and the coupling that matters runs between them.

Band choice is not the start of anything, and it is not one decision. It is two decisions on two clocks, and separating them is what makes the joint problem statable:

Range is a commitment. Which band class you fly determines contact range, which determines clustering, which fixes the stops and the route. That is settled before takeoff and cannot be revised mid-hop without re-planning.

Rate is revisable. Which band you actually transmit on at a given stop depends on the phase you find when you arrive. That is settled per hop, and it is where your prototype already operates.

The hard part — and the reason a decomposed heuristic struggles here — is that the slow commitment bounds the fast decision, while the payoff of the slow commitment is only observable through the fast one. You commit to a clustering under an assumed range, then discover stop by stop whether the rate you actually get pays for the route that range implied. A two-timescale problem with a commitment stage is a much better home for a value function than a permutation of contacts that will all be visited anyway.

The three cuts sit in three different places in that picture — one in each loop, and one on the path between them.

  1. 1
    Plan time · the commitment

    There is no contact-band decision to make

    Precision matters here, and the earlier framing was loose. Today's L1 selects a band for the backhaul — mule to base station, once per mission, at the dock. The device-contact link is a different radio entirely, and it has no band variable at all: rf_range_m is a fixed 60 m constant. So the proposal is not "wire L1's band to range." It is introduce a second band decision on the contact link and let that one set range. Without it, the plan-time loop has nothing to commit to — nothing the RF layer chooses can change who shares a stop.

    experiments/exp4/driver.py:75,155 — rf_range_m default 60.0 · L1 record §0, backhaul only

  2. 2
    Flight time · the reaction

    The band decision sits on the wrong clock

    L1 re-selects per mission; the phase that matters is the one at arrival. A once-per-sortie commitment cannot respond to a channel that moves during transit, which is precisely the effect the next figure measures. And the prior that reaches the selector is mean_chosen_snr_db — one mission-mean scalar, identical across every candidate in a ranking batch, which is one reason four of the eleven feature slots are constant.

    channel.py → MuleConfig.rf_prior_snr_db → selector/features.py:49 slot 10 · L2 record §4.6

  3. 3
    The return path · flight time → plan time

    What flight time learns never reaches the plan

    Two halves of one broken return. S3b does model travel at cruise_speed_m_s and does test the mission budget — but dwell is a constant session_time of 1.0 s, so a slow band and a fast band cost the same time at a stop. The clock the mule carries back is therefore computed from an assumption, not a measurement. And the one edge that does carry realised state upward, _remaining_is_feasible(), only aborts; it never re-plans. So the plan-time loop neither receives a true cost nor acts on the drift it can already see — which is why Q2's re-planning and this cut are the same piece of work.

The sharpest case for learning

You already prototyped the right structure

hermes_rl/drone_env.py has the joint action — (waypoint, base station, channel) — chosen at every step, with transit consuming time and carrying no transfer, and alpha > beta so the time-varying term dominates distance. That is the flight-time clock, and it is already the right one. Put its constants against a real four-stop sortie and the cost of running the band decision on the plan-time clock instead becomes arithmetic:

This is the strongest RL argument available to you, and it is the one to build the experiment around: the agent must choose a destination based on what the channel will be when it gets there. Value functions exist for exactly that, and the picture also shows why the two clocks cannot simply be merged — the arrival times that set the phase are themselves a product of the plan-time route, so moving the band decision to flight time does not remove the commitment, it makes the commitment consequential.

Make the ratio of transit time to channel period an explicit swept parameter. It is the knob that turns the learned policy's advantage on and off: shrink transit toward zero and the committed band is still correct on arrival, so the heuristic wins; stretch it past a full cycle and the observation at takeoff carries no information at all. A sweep over that ratio produces a crossover curve rather than a single contested number — and it is the same shape of result as your A1/A2 surface.

The five questions

Answers

Q1

Should we include a trajectory navigation layer?

Yes — half of it

Split what the doc lists. Distance and order: add. That is travel cost, and Exp 4 currently has none of it — no flight budget, travel time, propulsion or communication energy anywhere in the path. You have the machinery in Exp 3's sim_env and in S3b; it is disconnected, not missing.

Environment and obstacles: don't. Obstacle avoidance is solved motion planning. A reviewer will say "use A* or RRT" and be right, and it adds no FL-relevant structure — an obstacle changes your path length, which you could obtain by sampling path lengths. It buys complexity without buying coupling, which is the specific failure mode you are trying to escape.

Q2

Should the navigation be dynamic?

Yes — one sense only

Dynamic in the sense of re-planning when the world diverges from the plan, not reacting to terrain. You already have the seed: MuleSupervisor._remaining_is_feasible() re-runs the S3b check from the mule's current pose and clock before each stop. Today it aborts. Make it re-plan.

Your L2 record already draws the boundary correctly, and it should survive into the paper: running out of time is a deterministic function of clock and geometry and can be foreseen; a random link failure is stochastic by construction and cannot. That is a property of the model, not a gap in it.

Q3

Should we jointly optimise RF band and navigation?

Yes — and this is the contribution

Yes, but the joint variable has to be the one that carries the coupling. Not "band and route tuned together for their own sakes" — that is still two objectives in a trench coat. The load-bearing structure is not one chain but two, hinged: demand → contact band → range → clustering → route → commit at plan time, and arrive → observed phase → band → rate → dwell → clock at every stop, with the committed plan flowing down and realised drift flowing back up. Restore cuts 1–3 and you cannot fix the clustering without assuming a range, or evaluate that range without simulating the stop-by-stop rates it makes possible.

The test for whether you built a system or a stack is a one-line ablation: cut the band→range edge and see whether the result moves. If it doesn't, you added a layer. Run it either way and report it — a negative there is far cheaper to discover now than in review.

Q4

How do RF and navigation connect back to the FL job scheduler?

Three edges, on both clocks

This is the best question in the document, and its answer is your architecture figure. It is a bilevel problem with a bidirectional interface — and the useful part is that the same three edges appear on both clocks, carrying different traffic. That is what makes it one architecture rather than two subsystems that happen to be adjacent.

Note that this preserves your architectural guarantee untouched — the hard gates still run before anything learned ranks anything.

Q5

Is this enough to justify RL over a cross-heuristic?

It justifies testing, not asserting

Honest answer: the coupling justifies testing whether RL is needed. It does not guarantee RL wins — and the useful move is to stop needing it to.

Whoever told you to check the cross-heuristic is right, and you should build it. But it is your baseline, not your threat. Without a strong joint heuristic, "RL helps" is unfalsifiable and reviewer 74C's complaint never closes. With one, you can state a boundary — and a boundary is a better result than a win.

The claim to aim for is not "RL beats heuristics." It is:

Here is where a decomposed heuristic provably stops working, and we locate that boundary empirically.

Your revision plan already has this instinct at §7.3 — "the learned selector is not necessary under unconstrained conditions; its benefit becomes significant when budgets are tight." And your A1/A2 result is already a paper written in that voice: the mule loses on a clean backhaul, wins decisively under jitter, reported as a crossover surface rather than a pooled mean. Do the same thing for RL and you inherit a rhetorical structure that has already survived scrutiny.

The test

Three properties — and which clock each one lives on

Design the experiment to measure these rather than assert them. Two of the three split by clock, and they do not have to give the same answer on both — which is the point, because the pattern of answers is the architecture decision, not just the justification for it. If all three hold and the cross-heuristic still wins, the paper is still good: it becomes when does learning pay for itself in mule-based FL?, which is publishable, honest, and much harder to attack than a contested effect size.

What the three properties are choosing between, drawn in the two slots the workflow left open:

This one does not split by clock — it is the join, which is exactly why it is the first thing to measure. Falsified by — gap ≈ 0. Then plan and react separately, and ship the heuristic. Read the (b) row as a decision, not a result. Its two cells are what tell you what to build, and the three outcomes are genuinely different projects:

Flat on both clocks — there is no temporal credit anywhere; build the joint heuristic and write the negative result. Live on flight time only — learn the per-arrival policy and keep a deterministic planner above it, which is a small, well-scoped build. Live on both — you need a hierarchical policy, plan-time options over a flight-time policy, and that is a substantially bigger commitment that should not be entered without this evidence in hand.

Keep the existing claim rule — bootstrap CI excluding 0 and paired Wilcoxon p < 0.05, with the multiplicity position stated explicitly. It is what makes the A2 result credible and it will do the same work here.

Baselines

Which capability earned the gain

The to-do doc frames the comparison as static baselines (FedCS, Oort) against a dynamic one. That axis fails before it starts. Oort is already adaptive — its utility updates every round with a staleness bonus — and putting it in the same bucket as a learned trajectory policy groups two things that share nothing. Worse: if HERMES beats FedCS and Oort and the win is attributed to being "dynamic," nobody can tell whether it came from choosing where to fly, from knowing about FL, or from learning. A reviewer will ask which. A spectrum cannot answer; a factorial can.

Two axes genuinely separate the candidates:

Decision scope. Does the policy choose the trajectory, or only the order of service along a route it is handed? Selection-only policies live in the target_selector slot and permute within a bucket. Trajectory policies choose the next stop; who gets served falls out of where you go.

Objective denomination. Is the reward in FL units or physical units? FL-blind: bytes harvested, age of information. FL-aware: statistical utility of an update, fits-the-deadline, update yield, round closure.

Three tests fall out of the grid, and the third is the paper:

Scope effect — is choosing where to fly worth anything? Read down the columns. Awareness effect — is knowing about FL worth anything? Read across the rows. Interaction — is having both worth more than the sum of having each?

That last one is the two-clock coupling claim in statistical form. If the coupling is real, trajectory control is worth more when the objective is FL-aware, because FL-awareness is what tells the trajectory which stops matter and when. A 2×2 with paired seeds tests an interaction directly with the machinery already in experiments/analysis/stats.py — difference of differences, bootstrap CI, paired Wilcoxon — and it is the architectural claim this memo says to lead with.

The RL question is nested inside the bottom-right cell, not the headline. HERMES against the cross-heuristic holds clock structure constant — both are two-clock — so it isolates learned-versus-fixed and nothing else. That is where properties (a)–(c) apply.

Nesting it is what protects the paper. If learned-versus-fixed is the headline and the fixed rule wins, the paper collapses. If the interaction is the headline and the fixed rule wins the nested test, there is still a result: the architecture is right, and a heuristic suffices to exploit it.

And there is already one data point. Old H2 — the DDQN selector — is (selection-only, FL-aware, learned), and it tied H1. Learning did not help when the policy could only reorder. The factorial tests whether it helps once the policy can fly. That turns a published null into a falsifiable prediction, and it is consistent with the diagnosis at the top of this page: the old action had no consequence to learn from.

What the other axis is for

When is the ranking signal obtained — pre-selection reporting versus retrospective — is the axis the baseline candidates document already establishes, and it is the right one for a different job. It is a capability axis, not a performance axis: it decides who is admitted to the grid. It is why FedCS runs degraded (its Resource Request polls clients before selection, which a mule cannot do) and Oort runs faithfully (its utility is cached from prior participation, which is exactly what a mule has). That argument belongs in Related Work as the justification for the arm list. It is not a column in the results.

Chen et al. — what it is, and what transfers

Model-aided Federated Reinforcement Learning for Multi-UAV Trajectory Planning in IoT Networks, Chen, Esrafilian, Bayerlein, Gesbert, Caccamo — GLOBECOM Workshops 2023, six pages, code public under BSD-3. Three UAVs harvest data from ten static devices in a 3D city grid. Three separable ideas: a learned digital twin (fit a channel network from real measurements, PSO-localise unknown devices, train in the twin); QMIX for multi-UAV coordination; and parameter averaging between UAVs every fifty episodes, which the title calls federated. Same group as Mestoukirdi et al., which is already cited as the closest architectural prior.

Two fidelity caveats that must be stated if it is used. Their action space is grid moves; HERMES's is waypoint-to-waypoint on the contact graph, so the code cannot run as-is. The honest port is a single-agent DQN over HERMES's graph with their observation design — per-device SNR, distance and remaining data for reachable devices, all mule-visible — labelled "DQN over the contact graph, after Chen et al.," never "FedQMIX." And their objective is bytes: a harvesting policy will over-collect from good-channel devices because it does not know a second update this round is worthless. That is the expected failure mode and what makes it a meaningful baseline — but the comparison reports update_yield and round_close_rate, never raw bytes.

As a paper. Three random runs, no statistics; the headline "three orders of magnitude" is read off a log-scale plot. The 1000× is by construction — a thousand simulated episodes per real one, with the simulator's cost never reported. And it compares RL to RL only: QMIX, IQL, model-aided QMIX. There is no heuristic baseline. Once device positions are known this is prize-collecting orienteering with a rate function, and a greedy partition would be the obvious comparator. It is not run. That is the exact omission step 5 of the sequence exists to prevent — and, per the to-do doc's request for compute cost, the honest line is that Oort is a formula, MAX-AoI is a sort, and this needs a thousand simulated episodes per real one.

One thing worth noticing on the way past: their safety controller — mask any action that leaves the UAV unable to reach its terminal, then let the policy choose among what is left — is the same pattern as S3b and the "learning cannot override hard constraints" guarantee. An independent group converging on gate-then-learn is a citation in your favour. Cite it alongside Mestoukirdi and Bayerlein et al. (IEEE OJCOMS 2021, the multi-UAV DDQN work Chen builds on and the notes do not yet list): three papers from one trajectory-RL thread, which fills the empty "Adaptive:" line in the to-do doc with the real candidates and the criteria they passed or failed.

Scope

This is E3, not a new front

Worth saying to the team explicitly, because it changes what the redefinition costs. Your holistic revision plan already records that E3 — the scheduling and scale experiment — is "the largest untouched block of work in this plan," and Freeze D4 already settles that E4 makes no RL claim and that the RL question belongs to E3.

So this redefinition is not opening a new thread. It is finally specifying the experiment your own plan reserved for exactly this question. The practical consequence: the ICNC submission does not have to carry it. E4's crossover surface stands on its own as an availability result, and the RL question ships with E3 on its own timeline.

One thing to protect while you do this: the honest framing that already makes the E4 result credible — HERMES is not a general improvement on federated learning; it is an availability mechanism, and it costs throughput when availability is not the problem. The same discipline applied to RL is what will make the boundary claim land.
