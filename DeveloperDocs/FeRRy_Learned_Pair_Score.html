The learned pair score (FQ)

What FeRRy's learned model decides during a flight, what it sees, what it is rewarded for, how it is trained, and how Study 5.5 decides whether to keep it.

Where it sits in a mission

FeRRy flies a drone (the "mule") from a dock to a set of hover points ("stops"), collects federated-learning updates from the IoT devices near each stop, returns to merge them, and flies a second pass to deliver the new model. The flight is straight legs between stops at cruise speed and a fixed 25 m altitude: FeRRy does no motion planning. Its "trajectory" is which stops, in what order, on which radio band.

Two clocks make those choices:

  • The plan clock, at the dock. FeRRy chooses a band class and a route together: it clusters devices into stops for each class, scores each (class, route) on mission time plus a penalty for devices left out, keeps only routes that fit the deadlines, budget and battery, and commits the best.
  • The flight clock, at each stop of Pass 1. The drone measures the real signal and makes one choice: which band to serve this stop on, and which stop to fly to next. This is the choice the learned score makes.

The decision

At each Pass-1 arrival the options are pairs: a band class to serve this stop on, and a next stop from the plan's remaining stops, or home once none is left. The count is the classes that cover the plan times the stops left: the probes saw up to 15 pairs at N = 12 and 21 at N = 24.

The mask: what the score may choose (decision 1 (a))

A pair is allowed only if all three hold:

  1. The band covers the plan. It reaches every device the plan's band reaches at this stop, at the measured signal.
  2. The next stop is in the plan. The score reorders the plan; it never invents stops.
  3. The rest of the flight still fits. After serving this stop on that band and flying to that stop, the whole remaining route, priced at the observed signal, still meets the deadlines, the time budget and the energy limit.

If no pair is allowed, the drone flies the fixed rule FX's pair and logs it. Ties go to the lowest row, so with one class the plan's order wins. After the choice, the chosen stop moves to the front of the remaining route, and the normal departure check still runs: if the route no longer fits, stops are trimmed (overdue devices kept first). The score never runs in Pass 2, which flies the committed order on the committed band.

Inputs: 36 numbers per pair

Each allowed pair becomes one row of 36 numbers (feature schema pair_v1, with three band classes). Some columns depend on the band only, some on the next stop only, some on both, and some describe the whole situation and are the same in every row. Every value is something the drone has measured or knows at the moment of arrival; nothing is read from the future. Times are scaled by T, the nominal mission length, and N is the number of devices in the mission.

The phase block exists because the contact channel's interference is a regular wave with period P_c: one reading cannot tell a rising signal from a falling one, so the score gets two readings and their timing. Two inputs the design once had are gone: a per-stop "value" (it equalled members ÷ N under equal data) and a clipped slack (it saturated).

Output and network

One score Q per allowed pair

The highest Q
Network

Library
numpy (no torch)

The same small network scores every row, so it handles any number of pairs: rows can come and go as stops are visited. The situation and phase columns repeat in every row, which is how the shared context reaches each pair's score. The chosen pair is the masked argmax; the drone serves this stop on its band and flies to its stop. Each decision is logged in the trace (mission_completed.pass_1_pairs) with the pair chosen, the mask, whether it agreed with FX, and optionally the Q values.

Reward

Each decision at stop k earns (decision 4 (a)):

rk = Gk − ct · Δtk / T
and at the flight's last decision, also − ccov · U
G_k
The value of the updates collected at stop k: each update's merge weight (examples × value × age discount, 0 past the cutoff), divided by n_ref·N, so one fresh device of standard size is worth 1/N. With equal data on every device, as in training, it is simply devices collected ÷ N.
Δt_k
Time from arriving at k to arriving at the next stop (after the last decision, to the end of the upload home).
T
The nominal mission length, the plan's own time unit.
U
The share of the plan's devices, weighted by the plan's coverage weights, left uncollected when the flight ends.
c_t, c_cov
0.1 and 1 by default. Study 5.7 sweeps c_t ∈ {0.03, 0.1, 0.3} × c_cov ∈ {0.25, 1, 4}.

So the score is paid for collecting updates, charged a little for time, and penalised at the end for skipping planned devices. There is no energy term (energy tracks time) and no lateness term (none of 1,148 probed collections was late). A "sortie" (one Pass 1, dock to dock) is the score's horizon.

Training uses expected availability. Whether a device answers is a random draw that does not depend on the pair chosen, and its noise is 10 to 100 times the time signal a decision moves. During training each targeted device is credited its probability of answering instead of the draw; evaluation and every reported number use the real draws.

Two other rewards exist for comparison: F·hand, the older hand-set reward ported from ContactSim and labelled as such (arm FQ-hand), and bytes, devices collected ÷ N, which is E3's reward.

Training

The score trains in FerrySim: the real FeRRy stack (driver, cluster, mule and device services) run in one process on a virtual clock, with a stand-in for the devices' local training. It is a double DQN whose replay stores each transition's next rows and mask, so the target takes a real maximum over the next decision's allowed pairs.

A trained score is saved as a checkpoint (arrays plus a JSON manifest). Its sha256 covers the arrays and a header holding the feature schema, the network, γ, the purpose and the learner revision. The mule checks the sha before flying it, and the runner refuses a checkpoint that is untrained, unscored, from a dirty tree, trained on another plan, or whose γ or reward does not match the arm's tag.

How it is judged: Study 5.5

The question is whether this choice needs to look ahead at all, or whether a one-step rule is enough. The rule was fixed before any training (decision 5 (a)):

  1. Sanity check. At γ = 0 the score must do at least as well as the better one-step rule (FX or greedy_1), within ε. The learner may be revised once at most.
  2. The sweep. 10 training seeds for each γ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 0.99}, each judged on the same 1,000 unseen episodes per N = 12 cell.
  3. Verdict. "Rising" if the best γ beats γ = 0 by ε with the confidence interval above 0 and Holm-adjusted p < 0.05; "flat" if every γ is within ±ε (equivalence test); otherwise "inconclusive".
  4. Replace FX only if the curve rises and the best γ beats the best fixed rule (FX, F, hyb, greedy_1) by ε. ε = max(0.01, 0.1 × the validation headroom) = 0.01 from the build's headroom check.
If the curve is flat, FX stays as FeRRy's in-flight rule and the null result is published. One signal to watch: on validation runs the one-step rule greedy_1 already beats FX in 5 of 6 cells (significantly at N = 12, 120 s), so the one-step baseline is strong.

FQ never leaves FeRRy's plan: the plan decides which devices and band class the mission is for, and the score only reorders the remaining stops and picks a band within that plan. E3 is the learned baseline without that structure, and M1 (one network over every pair, no plan) is built only if Study 5.5 keeps a learned score.
