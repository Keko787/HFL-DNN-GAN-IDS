\section{\sysname{} Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\sysname{} sustains federated learning across regions that a stationary network
cannot reach, by flying an autonomous mule (UAV) between edge devices and an
edge server. We describe the design by \emph{decision clock} rather than by
layer, because the two decisions that shape most of its behavior each span the
radio, scheduling and learning layers.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Setting, Layer Roles, and Decision Clocks}
\label{sec:method:roles}

\paragraph{Setting}
A cluster has one edge server, one or more mules, and static field devices whose
positions the server's registry holds. The server gives each mule a disjoint
slice $\mathcal{M}$ of the registry for the trial; with $K>1$ mules the slices
are angular sectors around the dock whose sizes differ by at most one, and the
mules fly concurrently from the shared dock. Devices train offline between
visits, so a contact is exchange only: the mule pushes the current global model
and pulls the device's prepared update. A \emph{mission} has two passes. In
\textsc{Pass 1} the mule collects updates along a route, merges them on board,
and docks to upload; the server merges across mules into the next global model.
In \textsc{Pass 2} the mule delivers that model to every device in its slice,
which gives each later update a known basis version, and so an age. A time
budget $T_{\mathrm{miss}}$ bounds Pass~1: the plan and every check in flight
require the predicted landing and upload to fall within $T_{\mathrm{miss}}$ of
takeoff. Pass~2 is not budgeted.

\paragraph{Learning task}
The devices train a binary intrusion detector on CICIoT2023~\cite{CiCIoT2023},
with 21 features scaled to $[0,1]$. Each trial draws 20{,}000 balanced training
flows, split evenly across the devices, and a disjoint test set of 6{,}000 flows
that is two-thirds benign, so labelling every flow benign scores 0.667. The
model is a multilayer perceptron (hidden layers of 64 to 4 units) trained with
Adam for one local epoch per model received; a device with no prepared update
trains inside the contact, at no simulated cost. The server evaluates each new
global model on the test set, stamped when the closing upload completes. Every
transfer is priced as $S=1$~MB, to represent a larger model than the 18.8~KB
one.

\paragraph{Layer roles}
The radio layer prices a link but never chooses among devices; the scheduling
layer makes every admission, band and route decision; the learning layer values
a collected update and the cost of a missing one (Table~\ref{tab:layer-roles}).
Hard gates (eligibility, the feasibility test, and the age cap) always run
before any ranking, so a ranking rule, learned or not, chooses only among
options the gates admit.

\paragraph{Two decision clocks}
On the \emph{plan clock}, once per mission at the dock, the mule chooses a
contact band class $\bbar$ and an ordered route together, because the band class
fixes the contact range, the range fixes how devices cluster into stops, and
the stops fix the route (Fig.~\ref{fig:clocks}). On the \emph{flight clock}, in
Pass~1, it can revise the band and the next stop from the signal it observes.
The two clocks are needed because interference varies with a period comparable
to one leg, so the band that is best at takeoff need not be best at a later
stop. What the flight observes returns to the plan: rate sets dwell, dwell
advances the mission clock, the clock decides feasibility, and infeasibility
triggers a re-plan. Every decision is a rule or a search over a declared score;
learning can enter only through the flight slot.

\subsection{Quantities Supplied by the Layers}
\label{sec:method:models}

\subsubsection{L1: Contact Link, Clock, and Backhaul}
\label{sec:method:l1}

\paragraph{Band classes}
The contact link offers a set $\mathcal{B}$ of three band classes that share one
carrier and differ in occupied bandwidth $B_b=N_{\mathrm{RB}}\times180$~kHz, on
LTE channel bandwidths~\cite{3gpp36104}: \textsf{wide} (20~MHz, 100 resource
blocks), \textsf{medium} (5~MHz, 25) and \textsf{narrow} (1.4~MHz, 6). With a
common transmit power and a noise floor that scales with $B_b$, a narrower class
reaches farther at a lower rate. Relative to a reference class with bandwidth
$B_0$ and slant range $R_0^{3D}$,
\begin{equation}
R_b^{3D} = R_0^{3D}\left(\frac{B_0}{B_b}\right)^{1/n},
\qquad
R_b = \sqrt{\left(R_b^{3D}\right)^2 - h^2},
\label{eq:range}
\end{equation}
with path-loss exponent $n=2.2$ and altitude $h=25$~m. Anchoring \textsf{wide} at
60~m gives planar ranges of 60, 120 and 232~m. At $R_b$ the mean SNR exceeds the
decoding floor by a shadowing margin $M_{\mathrm{sh}}=5.1$~dB, so a device at the
edge of its class is reachable with probability 0.9 under shadowing alone.

\paragraph{Rate and dwell}
The mean SNR at distance $d$ is
$\bar\gamma_b(d)=\gamma_{\mathrm{floor}}+M_{\mathrm{sh}}+10\,n\log_{10}(R_b^{3D}/d^{3D})$,
with $\gamma_{\mathrm{floor}}=-6.7$~dB (CQI~1). The rate is the smaller of a CQI
spectral efficiency and the Shannon rate on the occupied bandwidth,
\begin{equation}
r_b(\gamma)=\min\bigl\{\kappa_b B_b\,\mathrm{SE}(\gamma),\;B_b\log_2(1+10^{\gamma/10})\bigr\},
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
for $\gamma\ge\gamma_{\mathrm{floor}}$ and zero below, with $\kappa_b$ an
implementation efficiency. Moving $S_j$ bytes takes
$\tau_{j,b}^{\mathrm{dwell}}(\gamma)=8S_j/r_b(\gamma)$; a Pass-1 session moves
$2S$ (push and pull), a Pass-2 delivery and the upload $S$ each, and the dwells
at one stop add. The channel acts on a contact only through whether a device is
solicited (a device below the floor on arrival is not, and costs no airtime) and
how long its session takes: the rate is read at the session's start, and a
started session always completes. The messages travel over a lossless local TCP
link that carries no radio effects.

\paragraph{Channel over time}
The realized SNR of device $j$ on class $b$ at mission time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\gamma_{b,j}(t)=\bar\gamma_b(d_j)+X_j(t)+A\sin\bigl(2\pi(t/P_c+\phi_b)\bigr)+\sigma_I\,\xi_b(t),
\label{eq:realsnr}
\end{equation}
with correlated shadowing $X_j$ ($\sigma_{\mathrm{sh}}=4$~dB, correlation time
7.4~s) and a per-class interference term~\cite{3gpp36777} of period $P_c=60$~s,
class phases $\phi_b\in\{0,\tfrac13,\tfrac23\}$ and noise $\xi_b$; in the
interference-limited (``jittery'') regime used throughout, $A=5$~dB and
$\sigma_I=1.5$~dB. Both terms come from keyed hashes, so two policies that ask for
the same value at the same time get the same value. The planner prices only the
mean $\bar\gamma_b$, since pricing the realized phase would be an oracle, so
realized missions run longer than planned; the flight clock observes
$\gamma_{b,j}(t)$. The planner also uses the outage probability
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{out}}(b,d)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{floor}}-\bar\gamma_b(d)}{\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}}\right),
\quad
\sigma_{\mathrm{eff}}^2=\sigma_{\mathrm{sh}}^2+\sigma_I^2+\tfrac{A^2}{2}.
\label{eq:pout}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Mission clock and energy}
Mission time is a simulated clock charged for transit at 5~m/s, dwell, a 1~s
listen window at a stop with a missing reply, the return leg, the upload, and a
30~s dock turnaround. Energy follows the same ledger,
\begin{equation}
E = P_{\mathrm{move}}\,(t_{\mathrm{transit}}+t_{\mathrm{return}})
  + P_{\mathrm{hover}}\,(t_{\mathrm{dwell}}+t_{\mathrm{listen}}),
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
with $P_{\mathrm{move}}=143.6$~W and $P_{\mathrm{hover}}=168.5$~W from the
rotary-wing model of Zeng et al.~\cite{zeng2019energy}. Flight is planar at
altitude $h$, energy counts propulsion only, and no battery capacity is enforced.

\paragraph{Backhaul}
The upload rides the best of three backhaul carriers and is lost with a fixed
probability (2\%). A lost upload is not retried: the server returns the
unchanged model, Pass~2 delivers it, and the planner, which records the devices
as merged before the upload, still counts them as served. The component
ablations' backhaul test and every cell of the radio study
(Table~\ref{tab:studies}) use a time-varying backhaul instead, whose SNR
swings sinusoidally and whose loss is logistic in the SNR; on the fixed carrier
it loses about a fifth of the uploads (F closes 79\% of its rounds there,
against 97\%). Only there does the radio layer's adaptive carrier controller
(F+L1) act, and since it observes the realized SNR that decides each loss, it is
close to an oracle. The backhaul never enters a scheduling decision.

\subsubsection{L2/L3: Demand, Deadlines, Ages, and the Age Cap}
\label{sec:method:demand}

\paragraph{Contact outcomes}
A Pass-1 contact is \emph{clean} when the device returns an update matching the
round, size and checksum the mule expects, \emph{partial} when the device
answers but refuses or fails those checks, and otherwise a \emph{timeout}.
Whether a reachable device replies is drawn each mission from its reliability
$\rho_j\sim U(0.15,1)$; a device whose reply is lost still receives the push and
adopts the model. A wall-clock session timeout (36~s at $N=6$) is recorded as a
timeout; at 0.75, 1 and 1.5 times its value the results were identical. To study
compute heterogeneity, a device can take a log-normal time to train, restarted
by each model it receives; an update not ready on arrival is a timeout that costs
no airtime. The plan does not price training time.

\paragraph{Per-device deadline}
Each device $j$ carries a fulfilment window $\Phi(j)$ and a deadline
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Deadline}(j)=t+\Phi(j)-\iota(j),
\label{eq:deadline}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the planning time and $\iota(j)$ the time since its last clean
contact. After each contact the window is scaled and clamped,
\begin{equation}
\Phi(j)\leftarrow\min\{\Phi_{\max},\max\{\Phi_{\min},\,\beta_{o}\,\Phi(j)\}\},
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
with $\beta_{\mathrm{clean}}=0.8$; $\beta_{\mathrm{partial}}=1.25$ for a partial
or a timeout after the device answered (a failed push, a lost reply, or no
update ready); and $\beta_{\mathrm{timeout}}=1.5$ for a device that stayed
silent, was out of reach, or was left out. Windows are in units of
$\sigma=\Tnom/10$~s, where $\Tnom$ is the median duration of a nominal two-pass
mission on the wide band (203~s at $N=6$): $\Phi_0=60\sigma$, $\Phi_{\min}=5\sigma$
and $\Phi_{\max}=300\sigma$~s. A clean contact re-anchors $\iota(j)$; a failed one
widens the window but leaves the anchor stale, so a device that keeps failing
grows steadily more overdue. A device the plan leaves out, or the mule drops in
flight, is recorded as a synthetic timeout.

\paragraph{Ages and coverage weight}
The \emph{plan age} $a_j=m-U_j$ counts the missions of the device's own mule
since the mission $U_j$ whose merge last used its update; a high plan age
strengthens the device's claim to be served. The \emph{merge age} $a_i=v-b_i$
of an update is the version $v$ of the model the mule carries minus the version
$b_i$ the device trained from, which the device echoes with its update; it
discounts the update in the merge. A missed contact relaxes a device's
\emph{window} but not its \emph{priority}, which enters through the coverage
weight
\begin{equation}
w_j=\max(a_j,1)\,(1+\mu_j),
\label{eq:covweight}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_j$ is the miss streak; under a plan, $\mu_j=a_j-1$, so $w_j$ grows
roughly as $a_j^2$.

\paragraph{Age cap}
A device is \emph{capped} when $a_j\ge S$. A pilot sets $S=2$, the fewest
missions that serve every servable device on 90\% of sampled layouts at the
stress budget. A stop's own deadline is the earliest among its uncapped members,
and a stop of capped members only is exempt from its deadline clause but not
from the budget. A trimmed route flies stops with a capped member first; a
capped device its stop cannot serve within the budget gets a one-device
\emph{hover stop} between it and the dock; and the plan key first compares the
ages of the capped devices a plan leaves out.

\subsection{The Feasibility Test and the Mission Clock}
\label{sec:method:gate}

One predicate decides whether a stop may be served next; it is the only
admission rule in the plan-mode system, applied to the committed plan, inside
the plan search, at every departure, inside the re-plan, and to the in-flight
choices. For a stop $s$ with members $\mathcal{M}_s$, reached from a state
(pose, clock $c$, energy) on band $b$,
\begin{align}
t_{\mathrm{arr}} &= c+\|\mathrm{pose}-s\|/v,\\
t_{\mathrm{fin}} &= t_{\mathrm{arr}}+\!\!\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_s}\!\tau^{\mathrm{dwell}}_{j,b}(\bar\gamma_b(d_j)),\\
t_{\mathrm{home}} &= t_{\mathrm{fin}}+t_{\mathrm{return}}+t_{\mathrm{upload}},
\end{align}
and the stop is admitted only if (i) $t_{\mathrm{fin}}$ meets the stop's own
deadline, (ii) $t_{\mathrm{home}}$ falls within the budget, takeoff plus
$T_{\mathrm{miss}}$, and (iii) the energy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy} stays within
capacity, if one is set. Every clause is monotone in the member set, so a stop
that fails whole can be reduced to the members that fit. The test prices the
mean SNR, so the realized channel can lengthen an admitted stop; the departure
check catches this after the stop, but nothing re-checks after the last Pass-1
stop, so a realized mission can overrun the budget.

\subsection{Plan Clock: Reach and Route}
\label{sec:method:plan}

At the dock the mule commits a plan $\mathcal{P}=(\bbar,\pi)$: the band class
flown in both passes, and an ordered list of stops, each reduced to the members
that fit. The demand is every device in the slice, with its deadline, plan age,
miss streak and weight.

\paragraph{Reach is a decision}
For each class $b$, a greedy anchor sweep clusters the demand into stops at the
class's range $R_b$, at a cluster's centroid when every member is within $R_b$
of it. A wide class serves few devices per stop at a high rate; a narrow class
can reach the field from one stop but dwells longer. No fixed rule chooses
between them, so the planner prices every class and commits the best.

\paragraph{Route search}
Candidate routes pass through the feasibility test stop by stop. Up to six
demanded devices, an \textsf{exact} search enumerates every ordered sequence of
stops and member subsets, pruning failing prefixes, and is optimal over what the
score prices. With more devices but at most six stops, it searches ordered stop
subsets, trimming each stop greedily; beyond that, a bounded local search
(2-OPT, then first-improvement moves, at most 2000 evaluations per class) keeps
planning deterministic. The empty plan is chosen only if no other is admitted.

\paragraph{Plan score and plan key}
Candidates are scored by
\begin{equation}
V(\bbar,\pi)=-\Bigl[c_1\Bigl(\tfrac{\Delta}{\Tnom}\Bigr)^{2}+c_2U+c_3L+c_4\frac{E}{P_{\mathrm{hover}}\Tnom}\Bigr],
\label{eq:planscore}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta$ is the duration of the whole two-pass mission on $\bbar$, $E$ its
energy, and
\begin{equation}
U=1-\frac{\sum_{\text{served}}w_j}{\sum_{\text{demand}}w_j},
\qquad
L=\frac{\sum_{\text{served}}w_j\,p_{\mathrm{out}}(\bbar,d_j)}{\sum_{\text{demand}}w_j}
\end{equation}
the weighted coverage shortfall and expected link loss, with $c_1=1$,
$c_2=c_3=N_{\mathrm{demand}}$ and $c_4=0.1$. The form follows FedEx's
route-dependent bound~\cite{bian2025indirect}, but the score is declared, not
derived: the constants are hand-set and the term in $\Delta$ is a staleness
surrogate. Because every plan that serves a device pays for a whole Pass~2, $V$
alone can prefer to fly empty, so the mule commits the plan with the smallest
key
\begin{equation}
\bigl(\kappa_{\mathrm{cap}},\;-\textstyle\sum_{\text{served}}w_j/\sum_{\text{demand}}w_j,\;-V,\;\text{class index}\bigr),
\label{eq:plankey}
\end{equation}
compared lexicographically, where $\kappa_{\mathrm{cap}}$ lists the ages of the
capped devices the plan leaves out, largest first. Time and energy thus break
ties among plans that serve the same weight, and the order of enumeration never
matters. A guard fold re-checks the chosen route before commit, and every
device the plan leaves out is recorded as a miss. No plan-time value is learned.

\subsection{Flight Clock: Band, Next Stop, and Re-Planning}
\label{sec:method:flight}

\paragraph{The flight slot}
In Pass~1, after takeoff, the mule can revise two choices per stop: the band on
which to serve the stop it has reached, and the next stop of the remaining
queue. With an empty slot, \sysname{} flies the plan's band and order unchanged;
this is F, the reference arm. The fixed filling, FX, is a cross-layer rule that
makes the two choices at different moments: on arrival it picks the fastest
class that still reaches every device $\bbar$ reaches at the stop, at the
observed SNR; at the next departure, after the stop is served and the departure
check has run, it picks the nearest remaining stop whose move to the front still
passes the feasibility test from the realized clock, or else the plan's next.

The learned filling, FQ, makes both choices on arrival, as a pair $(b,s)$ ranked
by a learned score. A pair is admitted only if the band reaches every device
$\bbar$ reaches at the stop, the next stop belongs to the plan, and the rest of
the flight still passes the feasibility test after serving on $b$ at the
observed dwell; if no pair is admitted, the mule serves on FX's band and keeps
the plan's order. The score is a masked double DQN over one feature row per
admitted pair (the current and next-stop SNR per class, the dwell, deadline
slack, plan ages, the clock and energy left, and the phase of the interference
cycle), trained in FerrySim on a per-stop reward: the merge weight collected less
a time cost, with the weighted shortfall of committed devices charged at the end
of each sortie. FQ thus picks its next stop before serving, from the priced
dwell, and FX after serving, from the realized clock.

\paragraph{Departure check and re-plan}
After each stop, the mule checks the remaining queue against the feasibility
test at its current clock. If the queue no longer fits, it re-plans the
remainder: stops with a capped member move to the front, the rest keep the
plan's order, and stops and members that no longer fit are trimmed. Every
dropped stop is final for the mission and recorded as a synthetic timeout.

\subsection{Aggregation: Mule, Cluster, and Delivery}
\label{sec:method:merge}

\paragraph{Merge on the mule}
Devices send the update $\Delta\theta_i=\theta_i^{\mathrm{after}}-\theta_i^{\mathrm{basis}}$
with its basis version. At the end of Pass~1 the mule merges the updates with
age-dependent weights,
\begin{equation}
w_i=n_i\,v_i\,s(a_i)\ \text{ for } a_i\le a_{\max,j},\qquad w_i=0\ \text{ otherwise},
\label{eq:mergeweight}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
M=\sum_{i\,\text{admitted}}n_iv_i,\qquad
\Delta_m=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i\,\text{admitted}}w_i\,\Delta\theta_i,
\label{eq:mulemerge}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is the number of local examples and $v_i$ a value proxy (uniform in
the evaluation). The staleness factor is FedAsync's hinge~\cite{xie2019asynchronous},
$s(a)=1/(a+1)$, and the cutoff comes from the device's own window,
$a_{\max,j}=\lfloor\Phi(j)/\Tnom\rfloor$, so the window that admits a device
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:deadline}) also bounds how long its update counts. Dividing by
$M$ rather than $\sum w_i$ lets a uniformly stale mission move the model by
only $s(a)$ times its mean; with current bases the merge is the plain
example-weighted mean, as it is for almost every merge on the mule in our runs.

\paragraph{Merge across mules}
At the server, each mule's partial carries its own staleness $s_m=s(V-v_m)$,
where $V$ is the server's version and $v_m$ the version the mule carried:
\begin{equation}
\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\,\frac{\sum_{m\,\text{live}}M_m\,s_m\,\Delta_m}{\sum_{m\,\text{live}}M_m},
\label{eq:clustermerge}
\end{equation}
with $\eta=1$; a partial is live if non-empty. The server merges as soon as one
partial arrives, so each upload closes its own round and no round waits on an
unreachable device. A mule flies Pass~1 with the model it received at its
previous dock, so with several mules the others' merges in between make its
partial stale: at $N=6$ with three mules, 160 of 198 merges applied a partial one
to three versions old, discounted to between a half and a quarter of its weight.

\paragraph{Delivery}
If Pass~1 collected any update, the mule flies Pass~2: it delivers the new model
to every stop of its slice at $R(\bbar)$, nearest first, on $\bbar$, skipping only
a device below the floor on arrival. Each device stores the model with its
version and resumes offline training.

\subsection{Mission Round, Cost, and Implementation}
\label{sec:method:alg}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One \sysname{} mission round}
\label{alg:mission}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Slice $\mathcal{M}$, model $\theta$ with version $v$, budget $T_{\mathrm{miss}}$, cap $S$
\Statex \textbf{[Dock: plan clock]}
\State Take slice $\mathcal{M}$ and the model $\theta$ (version $v$) received at the previous dock; compute demand, weights $w_j$, capped set
\For{each band class $b\in\mathcal{B}$}
  \State $\textit{stops}_b\gets$ cluster demand at $R_b$ and add hover stops for capped devices
  \State $\mathcal{P}_b\gets$ \textsc{Search}$(\textit{stops}_b)$ under the feasibility test
\EndFor
\State $(\bbar,\pi)\gets$ plan with the smallest key (Eq.~\ref{eq:plankey}); guard fold; commit; record left-out devices as misses
\Statex \textbf{[Pass 1: flight clock]}
\For{each stop $s_k$ in the queue}
  \State Fly to $s_k$; observe $\gamma_{b,j}(t)$ for all classes
  \State Band $b_k$: $\bbar$ (F); fastest covering class (FX); with $s_{k+1}$, best admitted pair (FQ)
  \State Serve members on $b_k$; update $\Phi(j)$ and $\iota(j)$ from each outcome
  \If{remainder does not pass the feasibility test}
    \State Re-plan by trimming; record dropped stops as timeouts
  \EndIf
  \State Next stop: plan order (F); nearest that keeps the remainder feasible (FX); the pair's (FQ)
\EndFor
\State Merge on the mule with cutoff (Eq.~\ref{eq:mulemerge}); fly to the dock; upload
\Statex \textbf{[Dock: server]}
\State Server merges across mules (Eq.~\ref{eq:clustermerge}) to obtain $\theta'$ with version $v+1$
\Statex \textbf{[Pass 2, if Pass 1 collected any update]}
\For{each stop in nearest-first order from the dock}
  \State Deliver $\theta'$ on $\bbar$; devices resume offline training
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Cost}
Training stays on the devices. The \textsf{exact} search evaluates at most 1957
stop sequences per class, and the local search at most 2000, so planning cost
does not depend on wall time; each arrival evaluates at most
$|\mathcal{B}|\times|\text{queue}|$ pairs, one feasibility fold each, and both
merges are linear in their inputs.

\paragraph{Implementation, scope, and FerrySim}
The prototype runs the server, each mule and each device as separate Python
processes that exchange messages over TCP, and the devices train the real model;
mission time, the radio channel and energy are simulated, not measured. Updates
carry integrity checks but no authentication; poisoning-robust aggregation and
privacy mechanisms are out of scope. The learned score, the learned baseline E3,
and the largest problems ($N=48$ and $96$) run in FerrySim, an in-process
simulator built from the same driver, services, planner, flight slot,
feasibility test, channel and clock. It omits model training (a device returns
the global model plus small noise), flies one mule, uses an additive deadline
law and no merge cutoff, and grows the field with $N$ in its scale cells, so its
$N=24$ is not the full system's.
