\section{Evaluation Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Against the State of the Art}
\label{sec:res:sota}

With one mule at the knee budget, F reaches $\tau$ in 179\,s and each of the
seven baselines needs 232--263\,s (Table~\ref{tab:exp5_headline}): F is
23--32\% faster, every comparison is a claim, and F gets there first in
73--85\% of paired seeds. FedEx with its own merge (D4$_{\text{FedEx}}$) is
slower still (296\,s) and misses $\tau$ in 3 of 40 trials. FX (168\,s) does not
differ significantly from F (11\,s faster, 95\% CI [3, 20], Holm $p=0.072$).
FQ (169\,s) does not differ from F or FX, and it beats D4 and E3 at the knee in
its exploratory family. Doubling the budget to 300\,s does not close the gap: F
flies the same missions, the baselines need 229--248\,s, F stays 22--28\% faster
and every comparison is again a claim. Under the stress budget the gaps narrow:
the FedEx route, with or without its merge, is significantly slower, and the other
baselines trail by 37--64\,s without a claim, staying within the budget by
leaving devices out. Below it (exploratory, 20 seeds) no baseline overtakes F: at
60 and 45\,s F takes 143 and 142\,s and FedCS, the fastest baseline, 175 and
171\,s, without a claim, while F reaches $\tau$ in 95\% of trials and overruns
29\% and 40\% of its missions. With three mules every scheduler, FQ included, reaches
$\tau$ in 53--64\,s except D4$_{\text{FedEx}}$ (111\,s), whose unweighted, one-tour-old
updates slow learning; the budget does not bind there, and at 300\,s no arm moves
by more than 1\,s. Without a mule (H0), FL over the degraded link merges
0.5 updates per round and ends at 0.693 accuracy, barely above the all-benign
0.667, against F's 3.4 and 0.837.

\subsection{Why FeRRy Is Faster: Reach}
\label{sec:res:mechanism}

F does not need fewer rounds (every arm reaches $\tau$ in 1.55--1.68 rounds,
with near-identical update yield); its rounds are shorter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:exp5_mechanism}). Every baseline flies the wide band, which
reaches a device only from nearby, and spends about 140\,s of a mission in
transit. F's plan chooses the narrow band in 83\% of missions, whose range
covers the field from about one stop: F spends 18\,s in transit and 106\,s
dwelling, and its missions are 21--25\% shorter. FX and FQ inherit this reach
(narrow or medium on 95\% and 97\% of missions; Fig.~\ref{fig:exp5_learning}b).
Pinned to the wide band, F reaches $\tau$ in 232\,s, D4's own time; pinned to
narrow, in 184\,s. (These times are descriptive: the band-pinning study's
pre-registered metric, the age of updates, does not differ.) The choice is near
the best, serving as many devices as an exhaustive oracle at the knee budget and
4 points fewer under stress, and it adapts (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp5_mechanism}c): as the
field fills and the budget tightens, F shifts to medium and wide, mostly wide at
$N=24$ under stress.

\subsection{Scale and Decision Cost}
\label{sec:res:scale}

At $N=24$ (Table~\ref{tab:exp5_scale}), F is significantly faster than H1 and D3
at the knee (749 against 951 and 911\,s), ties the FedEx route (752\,s), and
beats it under the stress budget (661 against 752\,s); at $N=12$ no comparison
in the table is a claim. Over the 40 seeds of the learned score's full-system
check, FX is significantly faster than F at $N=12$ at the knee (428 against
461\,s, Holm $p=0.032$) but not under stress, and FQ (454 and 385\,s) does not
differ from FX (428 and 385\,s).
Planning stays cheap (Table~\ref{tab:exp5_cost}): alone on one host, a plan
takes 0.04\,s at $N=6$ and 1.5\,s at $N=96$ (p95 2.0\,s). In FerrySim the plan
serves 54--59\% of the devices at each scale's knee budget up to $N=96$, and at
$N=12$ more mules cut F's time to $\tau$ from 486\,s to 226\,s with two and
151\,s with three (Table~\ref{tab:exp5_mules}).

\subsection{The Design Claims}
\label{sec:res:claims}

Nine tests hold (Table~\ref{tab:exp5_claims}). Without the coverage term the
round-close rate falls from 98\% to 73\% (C2). FedBuff's buffering slows learning
in five of its six cells: at the knee it reaches $\tau$ in 65\% of trials and, on
the 26 seeds where both do, takes 375\,s against 157\,s. The selection
literature's deadline forms miss more deadlines, 49\% against 43\% under stress
and 44\% against 43\% at the knee (C3). At $N=12$ the per-stop rule beats the plan
alone at the knee (C4). Under the stress budget FeRRy's age of updates is 0.78
missions, against 1.06 for MAX-AoI, 1.07 for the Whittle index and 1.10 without
the coverage term (C5). Over eight missions the gap widens, to 1.22 against 1.64,
1.66 and 1.59, while the FedEx route, which never skips a device, stays ahead of
F without a claim at both horizons (0.72 and 1.13). On the time-varying backhaul the
adaptive controller lowers the age of updates from 0.88 to 0.69, close to the most adaptation
can give, since the controller sees the SNR that decides each loss. Other parts
show no measurable effect here: pinning the band leaves the age of updates
unchanged in all twelve cells, including a shorter narrow-band reach and more
devices beyond the wide band's reach (its effect is on time); the dwell term
changes nothing, nor does the age cap over four or eight missions; and the
plain-mean and Async-HFL merges tie FeRRy's because collected updates almost
always have merge age zero, while FedProx is not significantly faster.

\subsection{Learning Inside the Plan}
\label{sec:res:rl}

The learned-score study is flat: no $\gamma$ beats $\gamma=0$ by the
pre-registered margin, and the best ($\gamma=0.75$) matches FX while trailing a
one-step greedy rule. In the full system FQ is again indistinguishable from FX
(C4) and, at $N=6$, from F, so FX is FeRRy's per-stop rule. The two decide on
different information (FQ before serving, from the priced dwell; FX after, from
the realized mission time), so this compares two rules, not two rankings. FQ
trained with one mule at $N=6$ and 12, and beyond that it falls behind
(exploratory): F leads it by 9\,s with three mules (95\% CI [3, 16]) and, at
$N=24$, by 148\,s at the knee ([103, 200]) and 17\,s under stress ([1, 37]).

The exploratory comparison sets FQ against E3, a single-agent DQN after Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2023model} that picks every next stop with no plan, on the same 40
seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp5_learning}). At the knee FQ reaches $\tau$ in 169\,s
against E3's 252\,s (difference 83\,s, 95\% CI [54, 119], Holm $p<0.001$ across
the eight exploratory comparisons); under stress it is faster over 39 shared
seeds (162 against 201\,s), but the CI includes zero. FQ inherits the plan's
band and spends 17\,s of a 157\,s mission in transit, against E3's 142\,s of
225\,s, and in FerrySim E3's held-out return falls 6\% below FX's while FQ's
matches it. The gain comes from the plan, not from learning, which supports the
two-clock structure (C4).

The study of when learning helps asks whether a learned scheduler gains when the
channel's period is tuned to the pace of its decisions. At $N=12$, over 40 seeds,
it flies FX against E3 with $P_c$ at four and two times FX's median time between
arrivals (108 and 54\,s under stress, 136 and 68\,s at the knee), beside a clean
channel. FX is significantly faster at the knee in all
three channels, by 78--89\,s (Holm $p\le0.002$), and leads under stress by
20--62\,s without a claim. E3 is never faster over the seeds both reach and the
CIs overlap across channels, though at $P_c=68$\,s it reaches $\tau$ in 39 of 40
trials against FX's 36. E3 flies the score it trained at $P_c=60$\,s, so this
shows that it does not gain away from that period, not that retraining could not.

\subsection{Robustness and Limitations}
\label{sec:res:robust}

With strongly non-IID data (Dirichlet $\alpha=0.1$;
Table~\ref{tab:exp5_robust}), every arm reaches $\tau$ in about half the trials,
and F stays significantly faster than H1 and D3 where both do; D4 and D5 trail it
by about 100\,s (exploratory). At $\alpha=1$ the arms reach $\tau$ in 65--75\% of
trials, and F stays significantly faster than D3 (321 against 444\,s) but not H1
(304 against 382\,s on the 13 seeds both reach, Holm $p=0.40$). On IID shards and
with skewed shard sizes every arm reaches $\tau$ in every trial, and F is
significantly faster than both (194 against 282 and 280\,s; 127 against 174 and
179\,s). FX's lead over F is a claim only under label skew (52 and 49\,s at
$\alpha=1$ and 0.1; 16 and 9\,s otherwise), and the plain-mean merge ties F in
every partition.

The radio study
flies the time-varying backhaul, so its default (F at 216\,s) is not the main
configuration (194\,s on the same 20 seeds); under its harsher channels F stays ahead of H1 without a
claim, and FX leads F on the default channel and with 8\,dB shadowing, by 24 and
31\,s, without a claim (Holm $p=0.081$ and 0.051). The adaptive carrier controller
gains more: F+L1 reaches $\tau$ in every trial on all five channels, closes
94--98\% of rounds against F's 73--84\%, and is significantly faster than F with
17\,dB of interference, at $n_{\text{pl}}=2.7$ and with 8\,dB shadowing (by 18, 62
and 45\,s; by 53\,s on the default channel, Holm $p=0.051$, no claim). Given to H1
it saves 15--81\,s, without a claim. These gains are near the most adaptation can
give, since the controller sees the SNR that decides each loss. Among the exploratory arms, D3, D4 and D5 mostly trail F, but
with 17\,dB of interference none of them differs from it (264, 276 and 315
against 255\,s), and at $n_{\text{pl}}=2.7$ the FedEx route leads without
significance (237 against 257\,s, reaching $\tau$ in 90\% of trials).

F's lead rests on the narrow band, so it depends on the size of the update. With
the model's own 18.8~KB update, at that payload's knee of 120\,s, F flies only
the narrow band, its missions last 61\,s against the 203--205\,s of H1, D4 and
D5, and it reaches $\tau$ in 59\,s, significantly sooner than they do (272, 239
and 243\,s); FX ties it. At 1~MB, 89\% of its stops are on the narrow band and
its lead over the three is 70--88\,s. Larger updates move it off that band
(exploratory, 20 seeds, at the 150\,s knee): at 2~MB 66\% of its stops are on the
medium band and its lead over H1 is 34\,s, and at 5~MB 99\% are on the wide band,
its missions match H1's (256\,s) and the lead is 47\,s. At 10~MB it flies only
the wide band and takes 344\,s, against H1's 322\,s and 315--382\,s for D1--D5.
No baseline differs significantly from F at 2, 5 or 10~MB, on time or on reach,
and every arm reaches $\tau$ in every trial; the training-time study's 10~MB
cells make no claims either. F's times at 10~MB jump with the mission that
crosses $\tau$ (91--177\,s in 7 trials, 371--658\,s in 13), so the CI on its
difference from H1 is 211\,s wide: the lead can no longer be detected, which does
not make the arms equal.

Two further limitations stand out. FeRRy's plan prices the mean SNR, so
under the stress budget 29\% of its missions overrun, by 33\,s on average
(median 20\,s; Table~\ref{tab:exp5_headline}); the FedEx route, which never skips
a device, overruns in 84\%, by 24\,s on average, and F keeps nearly its update
yield with fewer stops. And the
plan does not price training time: when devices take time to train, F's yield
falls from 3.5 to 0.7 updates per round over the same 20 seeds and it reaches $\tau$ in only 70--75\% of
trials, while H1, D3, D4 and D5 keep 85--100\%. The loss belongs to the small
updates: at 18.8~KB and 1~MB F reaches $\tau$ in 70--80\% of trials and closes
40--47\% of rounds, and at 10~MB, on the wide band, 95\% and 80--82\%.
