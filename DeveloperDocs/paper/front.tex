\begin{abstract}
Federated Learning is increasingly used as a paradigm for preserving security and privacy in applications that operate on distributed systems. However, federated learning requires that every federated device can reach a central server during the federation process, which is not guaranteed in contested-field deployments such as disaster zones involving a UAV fleet. In such use cases, the radio access network primarily determines the range and strength of signals by modulating time budget and channel quality allocated for the UAVs. Devices that are out of range or poorly connected are skipped repeatedly or never reached at all, leading to repeated failures in federated learning. To address this challenge, the paper presents FeRRy, a Federated RF-aware Routing framework that jointly makes decisions about radio channel selection, task routing, and resource aggregation. FeRRy methodology is represented through three key components. First, a band-aware mission planner that selects the radio band and route before each UAV flight. Second, an online re-planning loop iteratively checks the channel, picks the band and next stop to match it retrospectively, and triggers re-plans for the rest of the route. Third, a hierarchical aggregation layer first merges updates on each UAV and then across the UAVs at the edge server, giving older updates lower weight. In addition, FeRRy improves system robustness by penalizing configurations that skip devices and caps access times on devices, while ensuring that remote devices are not left disconnected. The prototype runs the edge server, the UAVs, and the devices as separate processes that communicate over TCP, and the devices train a real intrusion-detection model on the CICIoT2023 dataset. We evaluate FeRRy on a testbed consisting of edge servers and UAVs, in which flight time, the radio channel, and energy are simulated to train a real intrusion-detection model on the CICIoT2023 dataset. We compare FeRRy against state-of-the-art schedulers including mobile-transporter federated learning (FedEx), Oort, FedCS, and a Whittle-index age-of-update scheduler using metrics such as target accuracy, round completion, deadline misses, and network update frequency.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Disaster response, wildfire monitoring, agricultural sensing, and tactical
field operations increasingly depend on edge devices deployed where fixed
network infrastructure is sparse, damaged, or unavailable. These devices
capture observations that may be difficult or impossible to reproduce, such as
sensor measurements from an isolated field site, imagery from damaged
infrastructure, or telemetry from vehicles operating beyond cellular
coverage~\cite{zeng2016wireless, song2022comprehensive}. Yet these are also the
environments most likely to experience denied, disrupted, intermittent, and
limited (DDIL) connectivity, where towers may lose power, backhaul may become
congested, and terrain or distance may prevent persistent communication with a
central server~\cite{gupta2015survey}.

Federated learning (FL) is well suited to this setting because devices train on
their local observations and exchange model updates instead of transferring raw
datasets~\cite{mcmahan2017communication, kostage2025hifins}. However, reducing
the amount of data transferred does not remove the need for connectivity. In a
DDIL deployment, the practical bottleneck becomes \emph{reachability}: if a
device cannot contact the federation server, its update cannot contribute to
the global model. As connectivity deteriorates, rounds increasingly close with
only a subset of participants, time out while waiting for unavailable clients,
or fail to produce a usable model altogether~\cite{li2020federated}. The
operational gap is therefore not simply making FL communication faster, but
allowing federation to continue when the network graph itself becomes
fragmented.

Autonomous UAVs acting as data mules offer a way to bridge these disconnected
regions by physically carrying model state between edge devices and aggregation
servers~\cite{zhang2025latency, arsalaan2024uavs}. Doing so, however, introduces
a coordination problem beyond conventional FL. A mule has limited flight time
and energy, so it cannot necessarily visit every device. A useful contact also
depends on whether an update is ready and whether the wireless link can sustain
the transfer when the mule arrives. Existing hierarchical FL, UAV-assisted FL,
physical model transport, RF-adaptive networking, and adaptive client selection
address parts of this problem~\cite{liu2020client, lin2023delay,
parasnis2023connectivity, yemini2023robust, zhang2025latency,
mestoukirdi2022uav, bian2025indirect, wang2018deep, cui2019multi,
nishio2019client, lai2021oort}, but they generally retain persistent
reachability, stationary aggregation, or a fixed communication setting that the
mobility decision does not control. A courier-based federation instead requires
these decisions to work together under a finite mission budget.

In this paper, we present \sysname{}, a Federated RF-aware Routing framework that
sustains federated learning across persistently disconnected regions by flying
UAV data mules between edge devices and an edge server. Its central observation
is that a mule's radio reach is itself a decision. A contact band with a
narrower occupied bandwidth reaches farther at a lower rate: it can serve a
whole field from a single stop at the cost of a longer dwell, whereas a wide
band serves each device quickly but only from nearby, so the mule spends most
of its mission flying between stops. Which trade is better depends on how many
devices need service, where they are, and how much mission time remains, so
\sysname{} chooses the band class and the route together, before each flight,
under the mission budget.

\sysname{} organizes its decisions by when they are made. On the \emph{plan
clock}, at the dock, the mule clusters the devices that need service at each
band class's range, searches routes that pass a single feasibility test (every
stop meets its devices' deadlines, and the mule lands and uploads within the
budget), and commits the plan that serves the most coverage weight, a weight
that grows with the number of missions since a device last contributed. On the
\emph{flight clock}, at each stop, the same test gates a per-stop rule that can
use the realized channel to pick a faster band or reorder the remaining stops,
and the mule re-plans the remainder when the channel has used up the plan's
slack. Updates are merged on the mule with weights that discount stale ones,
and across mules at the server, so rounds close with the updates that arrive
instead of waiting on an unreachable device. Learning can enter in one place, a
learned per-stop score, and the hard gates always run before it. We evaluate
three variants that share the plan: F commits it in flight, FX adds the fixed
per-stop rule, and FQ the learned score.

We implement \sysname{} as a prototype in which the server, each mule and each
device are separate processes that exchange messages over TCP and the devices
train a real intrusion-detection model on CICIoT2023~\cite{CiCIoT2023}, with
mission time, the radio channel and energy simulated. We compare it with seven
whole schedulers, our earlier staged heuristic and six drawn from the
age-of-information, client-selection, UAV-learning and model-transport
literature, on paired seeds and under an analysis plan fixed before the studies
ran. With six devices, one mule and a moderate mission budget, \sysname{}
reaches the target accuracy 27--31\% sooner than each of the seven baselines. The gain comes from shorter rounds, not
fewer: \sysname{}'s plan chooses the long-reach narrow band in 89\% of missions
and cuts the transit between stops from about 140\,s to 15\,s, and pinned to
the wide band it is no faster than FedEx's route. Under tight budgets it keeps
updates fresher than age-of-information and Whittle-index schedulers, and
without its coverage term the share of rounds that close at twelve devices
falls from 99\% to 74\%.
Several design elements show no measurable effect at our settings, and we
report them as such. Among them is learning: FQ performs like F and FX, so the
learned score adds nothing to the fixed rule. In an exploratory comparison,
however, FQ reaches the target 36\% sooner than a learned scheduler that has no
plan, which locates the gain in the plan rather than in learning. We also measure
where \sysname{} falls short: it plans on the mean channel, so it overruns a
tight budget in 30\% of missions, and it does not price training time, so it
becomes less reliable than simpler baselines when devices need time to train.

The contributions of this paper are:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Reach as a mission decision.} We formulate model transport by
data mules so that the contact band class, which sets how far a mule reaches
and how fast it transfers, is chosen jointly with the route under a mission
budget, and we give a planner that searches both under one feasibility test.

\item \textbf{A two-clock design with bounded learning.} The same feasibility
test gates a dock-time plan and an in-flight per-stop decision, with per-device
deadline windows that adapt to contact outcomes, a coverage weight and age cap
that keep devices from being starved, and staleness-weighted merges on the mule
and at the server. A learned component can rank only the options the gates
admit.

\item \textbf{A prototype and a simulator built from the same code.} The
multi-process prototype trains a real model at every contact, and FerrySim runs
the same planner, flight slot and channel in-process, to train learned
components and to plan for up to 96 devices.

\item \textbf{A pre-registered comparison with the state of the art.} We test
\sysname{} against seven whole schedulers with paired seeds and multiplicity
correction, and report where it wins, why (its band choice), which parts of its
design have no measurable effect, and where it falls short.

\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related_works}

Federated learning reduces centralized data movement by allowing participants to train locally and exchange model updates rather than raw datasets. FedAvg established this model-averaging paradigm, while FedProx improved robustness to statistical and systems heterogeneity \cite{mcmahan2017communication,li2020federated}. Hierarchical approaches subsequently introduced intermediate aggregation to reduce communication cost and accommodate heterogeneous edge environments. Aggregation-oriented hierarchical FL performs partial aggregation at edge servers before cloud synchronization, while delay-aware hierarchical FL explicitly accounts for communication delay between edge and cloud tiers \cite{liu2020client,lin2023delay}. Connectivity-aware semi-decentralized FL further exploits time-varying device-to-device links, and collaborative relaying allows well-connected clients to forward updates for clients experiencing intermittent server connectivity \cite{parasnis2023connectivity,yemini2023robust}. These approaches improve FL under unreliable communication, but they still rely on a communication graph in which model state is eventually forwarded through wireless links. FeRRy targets the more disruptive case in which such an end-to-end path may not exist and model state must instead be physically transported between isolated participants.

UAV-assisted FL introduces mobility into the learning infrastructure, but the role assigned to the UAV varies considerably. A UAV may operate as a mobile FL server whose routing is optimized with bandwidth, computation, and transmit power \cite{zhang2025latency}, or as a mobile orchestrator that visits discrete locations while optimizing routing and device scheduling altogether across multiple FL communities \cite{mestoukirdi2022uav}.  These systems use mobility to restore or improve communication between ground participants and the learning infrastructure. The closest architectural precedent to FeRRy is FedEx, which removes direct client-server connectivity and uses mobile transporters to physically carry model information \cite{bian2025indirect}. FedEx establishes physical model transport and relates FL performance to transporter travel time, but assumes preplanned tours and fixed communication conditions without adapting service to changing RF conditions, per-device deadlines, or in-mission contact opportunities. FeRRy extends this setting by treating reachability, service time, participant urgency, and communication conditions as part of the mission decision itself.

Another research direction studies UAV trajectory planning together with communication-aware data collection. Earlier formulations have demonstrated that trajectory and communication should not be optimized independently, and should jointly optimize multi-UAV trajectories, user scheduling, association, and transmit power for wireless service \cite{wu2018joint}, while more recent learning-based approaches move these decisions online. Multi-UAV controllers have used SNR, reachability, remaining device data, relative distance, and battery state to learn trajectories \cite{chen2023model}, and subsequent work has learned 3-D trajectories and device scheduling \cite{gao2024federated}, incorporated collection deadlines, link quality, and energy constraints through MAPPO \cite{mondal2025deep}, further optimized trajectory and task offloading under AoI, latency, and energy objectives \cite{wang2025joint}, and considered battery and interference constraints in federated-RL data collection \cite{kumar2026battery}. RF adaptation has likewise been studied through deep reinforcement learning for dynamic multichannel access \cite{wang2018deep} and multi-agent resource allocation over users, power levels, and subchannels in UAV networks \cite{cui2019multi}. These studies establish that communication-aware, and energy-aware multi-UAV trajectory learning is already well studied. FeRRy therefore does not claim RF-aware trajectory planning itself as novel, instead, its distinction is that the communication choice directly changes the feasibility and duration of transporting FL updates, while participant urgency determines which contact is worth serving next.

Client-selection and freshness-aware scheduling provides another part of this coordination problem. Existing methods select participants according to round deadlines and resource availability \cite{nishio2019client}, favor clients with higher training loss \cite{cho2022towards}, or use statistical utility and participation history observed in previous rounds \cite{lai2021oort}. This distinction between pre-selection and historical information is particularly important for physical data muling because isolated participants cannot continuously report their current state before the UAV reaches them. Related UAV-FL work has further balanced update age and data value under unstable connectivity \cite{cui2023data}, incorporated freshness into fairness-oriented scheduling \cite{zhu2024fairness}, required devices to be served within a bounded number of slots \cite{zhai2024uav}, and increased the priority of previously failed or unscheduled devices \cite{mestoukirdi2022uav}. These studies establish age-aware ranking and mechanisms to reduce starvation. FeRRy instead focuses on how these concerns interact with physical transport cost, where servicing an old or valuable update also requires flight time, contact time, sufficient radio reach, and remaining mission budget. 

Finally, asynchronous and hierarchical FL research addresses model-version mismatch when participant updates arrive at different times. Prior work has buffered asynchronous client updates before applying server-side aggregation, reducing sensitivity to individual stragglers while remaining compatible with secure aggregation \cite{nguyen2022federated}. Other hierarchical approaches perform asynchronous aggregation across both gateway and cloud tiers while jointly considering device selection and gateway association in heterogeneous IoT environments \cite{yu2023async}. These methods assume that updates are delivered through the network, whereas physical data muling introduces travel time as an additional source of staleness. FeRRy therefore treats aggregation as part of the mission lifecycle, where the same age and deadline information is used to determine whether a participant to be served also influences whether its transported update remains useful when the mule returns. The key distinction is thus the coupling of physical admission, service order, RF-dependent contact feasibility, and update freshness, rather than a new aggregation mechanism in isolation.

Table~\ref{tab:ferry-comparison} shows that prior work addresses individual parts of this problem, including FL-aware participant selection, freshness-aware scheduling, UAV trajectory planning, RF adaptation, and physical model transport. However, even the closest transporter-based approach does not address RF-dependent reach and service time with participant deadlines and in-mission route adaptation. FeRRy addresses this gap by using communication conditions, mobility, and FL-update urgency to plan and revise model transport under a finite mission budget.
